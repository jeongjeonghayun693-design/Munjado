A Screen That Teaches

Munjado, “pictures of characters,” is a genre of Korean folk painting that flourished from the late eighteenth century into the early twentieth. Each character is enlarged until its strokes become a stage for the emblems of a moral parable.

The hyoje variant depicts eight Confucian virtues and stood in the household as a perpetual, didactic reminder of one's obligations.

Seokgang Hwang Seung-gyu

石岡 黃昇奎

Born on 5 November 1886, the only son of Hwang Geon and a mother of the Gimnyeong Kim clan. He died on 12 May 1962, in his seventy-sixth year, having become one of the very few Korean folk painters whose name history has preserved.

A Man of Three Names

Most minhwa painters were anonymous artisans. Hwang is a rare exception, yet even his name is layered.

The House on the River

Hwang's birthplace stands in Dongmak-ri, Geundeok-myeon, beside National Route 7 on the Samcheok coast.

He built the house with his own hands and raised its walls from stones gathered along the riverbank, a fitting monument to a man whose talent lay in his fingertips.

Across that same river, in Bunam-ri, lived the family of painters who would become his teachers. Geography, as much as temperament, set the course of his life.

Two villages, one river, and a lineage of painters passed hand to hand.

Prodigious Memory, Nimble Hands

Accounts collected in Samcheok portray an exceptionally gifted and gregarious man.

  • An autodidact at the board. He taught himself janggi and baduk, and was said to have no equal in either.
  • Uncanny dexterity. He could twist strips of hanji into cord and weave a seed basket (紙繩) in the few minutes he spent in the privy.
  • A born raconteur. His banter commanded any gathering; once he began a story, it seemed never to end.
  • Tenacious curiosity. His appetite for learning carried him well beyond what his teachers could give.

Yi Gyu-hwang, eighteen years Hwang's senior and known locally as “Old Yi Chosi,” descended from a family that had moved from Pyeonghae in Uljin and painted for generations. Shaped by Yi Hui-su, he consolidated the Samcheok style and taught it to his son Yi Jong-ha and to Hwang. Scholars note that Hwang drew on both elder masters before forging a manner of his own.

A Prolific Oeuvre

80%of his surviving folk paintings are munjado, from a body of work numbering several hundred screens.

After his apprenticeship he advanced largely by self-study, and his range was considerable. Yet the moral screen of eight characters remained the genre with which his name became synonymous.

Line and Colour

The Samcheok encyclopedia locates his distinction in two things: line and colour. His contours are supple and unhurried, so that his figures appear ample and harmonious. His pigments are emphatic, drawn from the palette of temple dancheong, and every leaf carries an inscribed poem.

The result is a screen at once vivacious and composed, in which picture and calligraphy are held in equilibrium.

More Than a Folk Painter

For decades Hwang was filed simply as a specialist in hyoje munjado. A 2024 study of his poetic landscapes (詩意山水圖), some made before 1920, argues that he was in fact a faithful heir to the orthodox tradition of brush and ink.

The same research finds that his landscape manner shaped later professional painters such as Seogo (石梧), and that munjado by these followers have often been misattributed to him. Through master and pupils alike, he helped give rise to a provincial idiom now called the Gangwon munjado.

A Living Legacy

  • In museums. His screens can be seen at the Samcheok Municipal Museum and at Chuncheon National Museum.
  • In scholarship. The Samcheok museum devoted a 2005 volume, Korean Munjado, to his life and work, and the National Folk Museum's journal published a study of his bird-and-flower and chaekgeori munjado in 2025.
  • In the classroom. The Samcheok Hyanggyo runs folk-painting classes in which students learn the eight virtues by copying his munjado.
  • In design. Researchers have translated his emblems into contemporary textile patterns.
